—Abstract

Content teams need a way to know which webpages are likely to lose traffic before it happens, not after. This project builds a machine learning model that predicts whether a webpage's search performance will grow, decline, or stay stable, using one month of page level search and engagement data from FlyRank's internship warehouse.

We compared a simple hand written threshold rule against a Decision Tree, a Random Forest, and XGBoost, evaluating each on precision@50 how often the model's top ranked "Declining" calls were actually correct. The hand rule failed completely (0% precision). A weighted XGBoost model reached 68% precision@50 and correctly identified 51% of all truly declining pages, a substantial improvement after we found and corrected a class imbalance problem in the model's initial results.

The final model outputs a ranked, decision-support recommendation list not a verdict. It is meant to help a content team decide which pages to review first, not to replace that review.

01Introduction

The decision this work supports

We are trying to predict whether a webpage's performance will grow, decline, or stay stable, so an SEO or content team can decide which pages to review first.

Getting this wrong has two different costs. If we miss a page that's about to decline, the team loses traffic without knowing. If we flag too many stable pages as risky, the team wastes review time on pages that didn't need it. There are usually multiple, tangled factors behind a page's decline a simple hand rule can't account for all of them, and would take far too long to encode by hand even if it could. That's the case for using a trained model instead of a fixed set of thresholds.

The target has three outcomes GrowingDecliningStable and the model is evaluated as a ranking problem using precision@k, since what a content team actually needs is a trustworthy, ordered shortlist, not just a raw label.

April contains roughly 30,700 pages with no March baseline — new pages that appeared mid-window. These were dropped entirely before labeling, since there's no fair "before" to compare them against.

Missingness, and what we did about it

Two columns had meaningful gaps. client_has_ga4 is False for roughly 31% of clients no GA4 (on-page behavior) data at all for that slice. Separately, gsc_avg_position is missing on 6.2M rows (~63.3%) of the full dataset far more than the GA4 gap so it was not used as a model feature.

No client names, domains, URLs, or credentials appear anywhere in this repository or in the figures below all identifiers are hashed, and reporting stays at the aggregate level.

03Methodology

Defining "declining" honestly

Labels come from the change in gsc_clicks between March and April, per page, using deliberately asymmetric thresholds:

  • Growing: clicks up more than +15%
  • Declining: clicks down more than −10%
  • Stable: everything in between

The asymmetry is intentional: catching a real decline early matters more than confirming growth, so decline is flagged at a lower bar than growth. Pages with zero March clicks can't be scored as a percentage change, so they're handled with a separate rule both months at zero stays Stable, zero moving to any clicks becomes Growing (decline is mathematically impossible from a zero baseline).

A page with 1–9 March clicks can swing ±100% from a single click fluctuation noise, not a trend. Those pages get their own label, Low Volume, rather than being forced through the percentage thresholds.

Features

Four features, aggregated per page over March: gsc_impressions, ga4_pageviews, scroll_events, and ga4_availability_pct the share of March days a page's client actually had GA4 running, which tells the model how much to trust that page's engagement totals versus treating a low number as genuinely low.

Validation design

Splitting was done with GroupShuffleSplit, grouped by client_hash_id, so no client's pages appear in both the train and test sets. This was verified directly by checking that the two client-ID sets share an empty intersection not assumed.

The recall problem the top-k score hid

Precision@50 only looks at a model's most confident calls. Scored across all 30,557 test pages, the unweighted XGBoost model told a different story: Declining precision of 0.58, but recall of only 0.16 it was missing roughly 84% of pages that were genuinely declining. Stable makes up about 75% of the dataset and Declining only about 3%, and with no correction the model leaned hard toward the majority class.

Fixing it with class weighting

Retraining with compute_sample_weight(class_weight='balanced') gave rare classes like Declining more weight during training. Both metrics that matter for this task moved together not a typical precision/recall trade-off:

Declining recall
16% → 51%
Precision@50
66% → 68%

Given that missing a real decline costs more than a false alarm (Section 1), the weighted model is the one selected for the recommendations below.

05Limitations

What this work cannot claim

These results are decision support, not verdicts. Precision@50 measures how often the model's top ranked pages are correctly flagged it does not guarantee any individual page's status, and should inform which pages a team reviews first, not replace that review.
  • Single month window. Features come from March 2026 only, predicting into April 2026 only the model has never seen a page across multiple consecutive months, so April's outcome could reflect something specific to that month rather than a stable, repeating trend.
  • Correlation, not causation. The model finds patterns associated with decline; it does not identify what causes it. A high scoring page should be reviewed, not assumed to have a known root cause.
  • Excluded and re bucketed pages. ~30,700 April only pages were dropped (no March baseline); 1–9 click pages became Low Volume instead of a forced trend label; zero click pages used a separate rule. Reported precision reflects the subset of pages with enough March activity to trend reliably.
  • Partial GA4 coverage. ~31% of clients have no GA4 data in March. ga4_availability_pct flags this, but low availability pages lean more heavily on gsc_impressions alone.
  • Thresholds are judgment calls. The +15% / −10% asymmetric bounds reflect a deliberate decision, not a boundary discovered in the data. A different reasonable threshold would shift every downstream number.
  • Uneven, untuned performance. Even after weighting, roughly half of real declining pages are still missed. Growing performs worse than Declining and wasn't specifically addressed. All models used default hyperparameters on a single 80/20 split, with no cross validation split-to-split stability is untested.
06Recommendations

Two ranked lists, built for different jobs

Both tables rank pages by decline confidence only page ID, client ID, and score, no ground truth label, since these are genuine forward looking calls with no known outcome yet.

Global top 50

Ranking every test page by decline confidence and taking the overall top 50 surfaced the highest confidence calls across the whole portfolio but all 50 rows belonged to a single client, simply because that client had the second largest page count in the test set (9,308 of the 11 total test clients). Client size was driving the list as much as genuine decline severity.

Top 5 per client

Ranking within each client separately and taking each client's own top 5 guarantees every client shows up all 11 test clients are represented, regardless of size. This is the view built for a team managing multiple accounts, so a smaller or newer client's real risk isn't hidden behind a larger client's page count.
